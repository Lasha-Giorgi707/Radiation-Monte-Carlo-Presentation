01 —

Why I Chose This Topic

The intersection of nuclear physics and urban planning is rarely studied at the undergraduate level — yet it is one of the most consequential questions a physicist can ask. Every city, every metro station, every hospital built near a radiation source is, whether its architects knew it or not, a radiation shield whose geometry was chosen without systematic optimisation.

"The question is not whether gamma rays pass through concrete — they do. The question is by how much, and how architecture can change that answer."

I chose this topic for three reasons. First, it is directly applicable to Tbilisi: the city's deep-cut Soviet-era metro stations provide a real-world case study for multi-layer shielding, and their curved arch geometry is, as this simulation shows, physically significant. Second, it gave me a reason to learn a technique — Monte Carlo simulation — that appears in every branch of modern physics, from particle collider analysis at CERN to medical imaging dosimetry. Third, the problem is simple enough to simulate on a personal computer yet rich enough to produce genuinely non-trivial results: the "sandwich wall" experiment and the angular incidence study both produce outcomes that pure analytical formulas cannot predict in complex geometries.

The Beer-Lambert law gives us an elegant closed-form answer for a single homogeneous slab under narrow-beam conditions. Real cities are not homogeneous slabs. This project is about bridging that gap.

02 —

What Is Monte Carlo Simulation?

The Monte Carlo method is a class of computational algorithms that uses repeated random sampling to obtain numerical results. Instead of solving a physics problem with a single deterministic equation, we simulate millions of individual random events and observe the statistical pattern that emerges. The name comes from the Monte Carlo Casino in Monaco — a reference to chance, randomness, and probability.

The Core Idea

Imagine you want to know whether a single gamma photon passes through a 20 cm concrete wall. You cannot know in advance — quantum mechanics forbids it. What you can know is the probability that it survives each centimetre. Monte Carlo simulation runs this probabilistic process for 100,000 photons simultaneously and reports what fraction survive. The result converges to the true physical answer as the number of particles grows.

P(absorption in 1 cm) = μ × Δx
— probability that one photon is absorbed in one slice

I(x) = I₀ × e−μx
— Beer-Lambert law: analytical solution for ideal case

Monte Carlo result → surviving / total particles
— converges to e^(−μx) as N → ∞, but works for ANY geometry

How It Works Step by Step

Step 01

Initialise

Create an array of 100,000 particles, all set to "alive" (value = 1). This is the radiation beam entering the material.

Step 02

Iterate

For each 1 cm slice of material, generate 100,000 random numbers between 0 and 1. Any particle whose random number falls below μ is absorbed (set to 0).

Step 03

Count

After traversing all slices, count the surviving particles. Divide by 100,000 to get the survival percentage.

Step 04

Repeat

Run for every material thickness from 1 to 40 cm. Plot the results. The curve reproduces the Beer-Lambert law — validated.

The power appears when you add complexity: different materials in sequence, curved geometry where each particle hits at a different angle, or multi-scattering effects. The algorithm handles all of these identically — just more loops and different values of μ.

03 —

Why Monte Carlo Is the Right Tool

The Beer-Lambert law is beautiful and exact — but only under conditions that never exist in a real city. It assumes a perfectly collimated beam, a perfectly homogeneous material, zero scattering, and no secondary radiation. Urban environments violate every one of these assumptions simultaneously.

"When the geometry becomes too complex for a formula, the physicist must become a statistician."

When Analytical Methods Fail

Consider the Tbilisi metro arch experiment in this project: each of 100,000 photons arrives at the curved vault at a different random angle, producing a different effective thickness. There is no closed-form solution to "what fraction of a photon beam survives a curved arch with uniformly distributed angles?" Monte Carlo solves this in seconds.

Five Reasons Monte Carlo Dominates Radiation Physics

Reason 01

Handles Any Geometry

Curved walls, layered materials, irregular shapes — the same probabilistic loop handles all of them. No new equations needed.

Reason 02

Models Quantum Randomness

Photon absorption is genuinely probabilistic at the quantum level. Random number generation directly mirrors the physics — it is not an approximation.

Reason 03

Statistical Confidence

With N=100,000 particles, the statistical uncertainty is ±0.3%. The simulation is reproducibly accurate to three decimal places.

Reason 04

Industry Standard

GEANT4 (CERN), MCNP (nuclear reactors), EGS4 (medical physics) — all major radiation codes are Monte Carlo. Learning the method is learning the field's lingua franca.

Reason 05

Accessible

A physics student with a laptop and NumPy can reproduce results that 30 years ago required a mainframe. The barrier to entry is now near zero.

What Is a Python Library?

A library (also called a package or module) is a collection of pre-written code that you import into your programme to gain new capabilities without writing everything from scratch. Think of it as a toolbox: Python itself is the workshop, and each library is a specialised set of tools inside it.

Without libraries, simulating 100,000 particles would require thousands of lines of manual code. With NumPy, it requires one line: np.random.rand(100000) < mu

Libraries are created by scientists, engineers, and developers worldwide and shared freely. This collaborative model means that a physics student in Tbilisi can use the same statistical routines that climate scientists at NASA use. You install a library once with pip install libraryname and then import it at the top of any script.

A sub-module of matplotlib that provides a simplified, MATLAB-like interface for creating figures. Imported as plt in most scientific code.
Analogy: the drawing board — you set axes, labels, titles, and call plt.show() to display.

Built-in math provides trigonometric functions (cos, radians) used to compute effective thickness at oblique angles. SciPy extends this for advanced statistics.

Material Attenuation

The attenuation coefficient μ (mu) quantifies how strongly a material absorbs gamma radiation per centimetre of thickness. It depends on the material's density and atomic number. All values below are for 1.25 MeV gamma radiation — the energy typical of Cobalt-60, a common industrial and medical source — sourced from the NIST XCOM database.

Why Wood and Plastic Matter in Urban Contexts

In radiation emergency scenarios — a dirty bomb, an industrial accident, a reactor leak — the population shelters in whatever building is available, not in a purpose-built bunker. Understanding the shielding provided by interior wooden walls and plastic panels is therefore operationally important. While wood and plastic are poor shielding materials compared to concrete, a person sheltering behind several layers of furniture and interior walls gains measurable protection from residual contamination and low-intensity gamma fields.

Plastic deserves special mention because high-density polyethylene (HDPE, μ ≈ 0.073 cm⁻¹) performs nearly as well as water per centimetre, and its rigid form makes it practical as a shielding panel. HDPE is already used as a neutron moderator in nuclear facilities. For gamma shielding alone, it is inferior to concrete, but combined with lead (HDPE slows neutrons; lead stops gamma), it is part of the gold standard in mixed-radiation shielding.

Angular Incidence — 0°, 45°, 60°, 90°

When a gamma-ray beam strikes a barrier at an oblique angle θ, the photons must travel through a longer path of material. This increases the effective thickness they encounter without any change to the wall's physical dimensions. The relationship is governed by trigonometry:

X_effective = Xphysical / cos(θ)

θ = 0° → X_eff = X / cos(0°) = X / 1.000 = X
θ = 45° → X_eff = X / cos(45°) = X / 0.707 = 1.41 × X
θ = 60° → X_eff = X / cos(60°) = X / 0.500 = 2.00 × X
θ = 90° → X_eff = X / cos(90°) = X / 0.000 = ∞ (total absorption)

A 90° angle of incidence means the ray travels parallel to the wall surface — it would have to traverse an infinite path length and is therefore completely absorbed. In practice, near-90° angles produce near-total attenuation even for thin barriers.

Architectural Implication

These numbers have direct consequences for urban design. A building facade placed at 60° to a likely radiation source provides the equivalent protection of a wall twice as thick — at zero additional material cost. This is why Soviet-era civil defence manuals specify angled shelter orientations, and why modern emergency planners favour stepped or wedge-shaped building profiles in high-risk industrial zones.

07 —

Sandwich Geometry & Urban Design

The "sandwich" experiment tests whether splitting a concrete wall and inserting an air gap changes its radiation performance. This has real architectural relevance: double-skin facades, ventilated cavity walls, and double-glazed windows all create air layers within building envelopes.

The Experiment

Two configurations are compared, both using exactly 30 cm of concrete total:

Variant A

Monolithic Wall

30 cm of solid concrete. All material is contiguous. The attenuation follows a single exponential decay from entry to exit.

Variant B

Sandwich Wall

15 cm concrete + 10 cm air gap + 15 cm concrete. The same total concrete, but the air layer physically separates the two slabs.

The simulation result is counter-intuitive: the sandwich wall performs marginally worse than the monolithic wall, because the 10 cm of air (μ = 0.00008) contributes essentially nothing to attenuation. The total attenuation depends only on the total concrete thickness, and the air gap replaces 10 cm of concrete with 10 cm of near-vacuum. The lesson is important: material matters more than structure for radiation shielding, unlike sound insulation where the air gap is beneficial.

"The sandwich wall is architecturally elegant and thermally efficient. As a gamma shield, it is simply 30 cm of concrete with a gap. The physics is unimpressed by the gap."

Where the sandwich geometry does help is in mass distribution: two separate 15 cm slabs are structurally easier to build at depth (metro stations, bunkers) than a single 30 cm monolith, and they provide redundancy — if one slab is compromised, the second still shields. The acoustic and thermal benefits of the cavity are also real. The conclusion is nuanced: the sandwich design is excellent architecture; it is simply not a radiation-shielding upgrade.

08 —

Conclusions

1

Material Hierarchy

Lead > Concrete > Plastic/Water > Wood > Air. Density and atomic number drive attenuation. Lead is optimal but impractical at scale; concrete is the urban standard.

2

Angle Multiplies Protection

A 60° incidence angle doubles the effective barrier thickness. Architectural orientation is a free shielding upgrade — no additional material required.

3

Sandwich ≠ Better Shield

Air gaps do not improve gamma attenuation. The sandwich wall performs identically to its total concrete thickness. The gap offers structural and thermal benefits only.

4

Monte Carlo = Right Tool

For heterogeneous geometries, probabilistic angle distributions, and layered materials, Monte Carlo is the only practical approach. Analytical formulas fail in all real-world cases.

The Monte Carlo method does not give a formula. It gives a fact — arrived at the same way nature arrives at it: by running the experiment an enormous number of times and recording what happens.

This project validated the Beer-Lambert law as a special case of the Monte Carlo result, demonstrated the physical significance of angular incidence, confirmed that the sandwich architecture is a shielding-neutral choice, and provided the first systematic comparison of wood and plastic as urban shielding materials in the context of a Georgian city's infrastructure. It serves as a foundation for more detailed simulations incorporating scattering, secondary radiation, and three-dimensional urban geometries.
